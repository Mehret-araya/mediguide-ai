## Overview

Diarrhoeal disease is the second leading cause of death in children under five years old, and is responsible for killing around 443,832 children every year. Diarrhoea can last several days, and can leave the body without the water and salts that are necessary for survival. Most people who die from diarrhoea actually die from severe dehydration and fluid loss.

Children who are malnourished or have impaired immunity as well as people living with HIV are most vulnerable to life-threatening diarrhoea.

Diarrhoea is defined as the passage of three or more loose or liquid stools per day (or more frequent passage than is normal for the individual). Frequent passing of formed stools is not diarrhoea, nor is the passing of loose, "pasty" stools by breastfed babies.

## Types of Diarrhoeal Disease

There are three clinical types of diarrhoea:

### Acute Watery Diarrhoea

Acute watery diarrhoea lasts several hours or days, and includes cholera. It causes fluid and salt loss that can lead to severe dehydration and death if not treated.

### Acute Bloody Diarrhoea

Acute bloody diarrhoea, also called dysentery, causes intestinal damage, sepsis, and malnutrition. It is associated with significant morbidity and mortality.

### Persistent Diarrhoea

Persistent diarrhoea lasts 14 days or longer. It can cause significant weight loss and is associated with malnutrition, particularly in children.

## Causes

Infection: Diarrhoeal disease can be caused by a host of bacterial, viral, and parasitic organisms. Infection is spread through contaminated food or drinking water, or from person to person as a result of poor hygiene.

The most severe threat is posed by:

- Rotavirus — the most common cause of severe diarrhoeal disease in children under 5 years of age
- Vibrio cholerae — the bacterium responsible for cholera
- Cryptosporidium — a parasitic cause of diarrhoea, particularly in children and people with HIV

Malnutrition: Children who die from diarrhoea often suffer from underlying malnutrition, which makes them more vulnerable to diarrhoea. Each diarrhoeal episode, in turn, makes their malnutrition worse.

Other causes include contaminated water sources, inadequate sanitation, and poor hygiene practices.

## Symptoms

The main symptom of diarrhoea is frequent loose or liquid stools. Associated symptoms may include:

- Abdominal cramps
- Nausea and vomiting
- Fever
- Blood in the stool (in dysentery)

### Signs of Dehydration

Dehydration is the most dangerous complication of diarrhoea. Signs of dehydration include:

- Decreased urine output
- Dry mouth and throat
- Feeling dizzy when standing
- Sunken eyes
- Skin that does not spring back quickly when pinched (in children)
- Unusual sleepiness or fussiness (in children)

Severe dehydration is a medical emergency. Seek immediate medical care if signs of severe dehydration are present.

## Prevention

A number of interventions can help prevent diarrhoea, including:

### Safe Drinking Water

Access to safe drinking water is essential. Water should be treated if its safety cannot be guaranteed. Boiling, chlorination, and filtration are effective treatment methods.

### Improved Sanitation

Access to improved sanitation facilities reduces the risk of diarrhoeal disease. Open defecation is a major risk factor for diarrhoeal disease transmission.

### Handwashing with Soap

Handwashing with soap at critical times — particularly after using the toilet and before handling food — is one of the most effective ways to prevent diarrhoeal disease.

### Breastfeeding

Exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life protects infants against diarrhoea. Breastfed children who do get diarrhoea tend to have milder illness.

### Rotavirus Vaccination

Rotavirus vaccines are available and recommended by WHO. Vaccination can significantly reduce the burden of severe rotavirus diarrhoea in children.

### Vitamin A Supplementation

Vitamin A deficiency increases the risk of severe diarrhoea. Vitamin A supplementation in deficient populations can reduce diarrhoea-related mortality.

### Zinc Supplementation

Zinc supplementation reduces the duration and severity of diarrhoeal episodes in children and reduces the risk of future episodes. WHO and UNICEF recommend 10–20 mg of zinc per day for 10–14 days for children with diarrhoea.

## Treatment

Diarrhoea can be treated and the lives of children with severe diarrhoea can be saved through the use of oral rehydration solution (ORS).

### Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS)

ORS is a mixture of clean water, salt, and sugar. It is absorbed in the small intestine and replaces the water and electrolytes lost in the faeces. ORS is safe, effective, and inexpensive.

WHO and UNICEF recommend a low-osmolarity ORS formulation. ORS sachets are available from health facilities and pharmacies in most countries.

### Zinc Supplementation

Zinc supplementation is an important component of diarrhoea treatment. It reduces the duration and severity of the episode and helps prevent future episodes.

### Continued Feeding

Continued feeding during diarrhoea is important to maintain nutritional status. Breastfeeding should continue throughout the illness.

### Antibiotics

Antibiotics are not routinely recommended for watery diarrhoea. They are indicated for dysentery (bloody diarrhoea) and for cholera. Antibiotic treatment decisions must be made by a qualified healthcare professional.

### When to Seek Medical Care

Medical care should be sought immediately if:

- Signs of severe dehydration are present
- Diarrhoea is bloody
- Diarrhoea has lasted more than 3 days
- The patient is an infant, elderly, or immunocompromised
- Vomiting prevents oral rehydration

## WHO Response

WHO works with Member States and partners to strengthen national diarrhoeal disease control programmes. Key areas of WHO work include:

- Promoting access to safe water and sanitation
- Supporting rotavirus vaccination programmes
- Promoting ORS and zinc supplementation
- Strengthening surveillance and outbreak response
- Supporting research on diarrhoeal disease prevention and treatment
